# Build a Replay Simulator, Forecast Before Deciding

No retail broker or exchange paper account is an exact replica of live trading, so the closest a solo Canadian developer can get is a **self-built replay simulator** that consumes live and recorded order-book data, runs on **NautilusTrader** (one strategy codebase across backtest, live-data sandbox and live), and uses broker paper accounts only to test order plumbing. Your priority markets rank in reverse order for automation: a Canadian resident **cannot send programmatic orders for TSX, TSXV, CSE or Cboe Canada listings** through IBKR Canada (blocked, citing a CIRO rule) or Questrade (API orders are partner-only), so Canadian exchanges suit research and human-approved swing signals but not a live automated loop, while US-listed securities through the IBKR API and crypto on Canada-registered exchanges are programmable. The easiest place to start is **crypto on Coinbase or Kraken public feeds**, where full-depth live data is free, markets run 24/7 and the recorder, matching simulator and validation pipeline can be built at near-zero cost, with US equities through IBKR as the first real-broker paper-to-live step. Your model premise holds only weakly: published ML results show out-of-sample R2 of roughly **0.4% monthly** and daily direction accuracy near **51%**, so the achievable output is a calibrated multi-horizon distribution with a small mean shift, not a point call like "-0.5% in 1 hour, +1.3% by tomorrow noon", and the 1-hour horizon is the hardest one for a retail data setup. Forecast-then-decide beats reinforcement learning as the core, because evidence for RL as an alpha source is thin while explicit sizing, cost and exit logic is auditable; RL earns a place only as a shadow-mode overlay or in order execution. The recommended stack is a gradient-boosted quantile forecaster with conformal calibration feeding a receding-horizon optimizer with fractional-Kelly sizing and a hard risk gate, executed through Nautilus, built in the phases laid out at the end.

## No platform is a true replica, so replay real data through your own engine

Every broker and exchange simulator reviewed admits it is not the real market. IBKR's knowledge base says a paper account simulates "not only the trader's functionality and the market, but also the role of the exchange" and that "there is no way to exactly replicate live markets" ([IBKR KB 252](https://ibkb.interactivebrokers.com/article/252)). Its paper engine fills from the top of the book with no deep-book access, always simulates stops, and rejects the unfilled remainder of a partially executed market order ([IBKR paper trading guide](https://www.ibkrguides.com/clientportal/aboutpapertradingaccounts.htm)); IBKR Campus adds that it ignores market impact, slippage, microstructure, price improvement, regulatory fees and dividends ([IBKR Campus](https://www.interactivebrokers.com/campus/?p=155866)). Alpaca's paper engine fills against the NBBO, gives a random partial fill 10% of the time, never checks order size against displayed quantity, and does not model latency, impact, queue position, price improvement or fees ([Alpaca paper docs](https://docs.alpaca.markets/docs/paper-trading)). Crypto venues are no better: Binance says its Spot Testnet prices and books are "independent of the live exchange" and that Demo Mode data is "realistic but not real" ([Binance Demo Mode](https://developers.binance.com/en/docs/products/spot/demo-mode/general-info)), and Coinbase's Advanced Trade sandbox is a static mock ([Coinbase sandbox](https://docs.cdp.coinbase.com/coinbase-app/advanced-trade-apis/sandbox.md)). Bybit's Demo Trading comes closest because its public websocket data is identical to mainnet ([Bybit V5 demo](https://bybit-exchange.github.io/docs/v5/demo)), but it needs a main-account login on an exchange that left Canada. These environments are useful for validating order lifecycle and API behaviour. Fidelity itself has to come from a local simulator replaying real order-book data.

NautilusTrader is the closest open-source fit and the only engine surveyed that spans all three asset classes on one code path. Its docs describe three environment contexts on one core, Backtest, Sandbox (live data feeds with virtual execution) and Live, and say the same strategy code runs across them ([Nautilus overview](https://nautilustrader.io/docs/latest/concepts/overview/), [live concepts](https://nautilustrader.io/docs/latest/concepts/live/)). The matching engine handles L1, L2 and L3 books, IOC/FOK/GTC/GTD/DAY and at-the-open/at-the-close time in force, OCO/OUO/OTO contingent orders, a `queue_position` option that tracks displayed quantity ahead of each limit order, a `liquidity_consumption` option that stops one displayed quantity filling several orders, tiered and volume-sensitive fill models, a latency model and pluggable fee models ([GitHub](https://github.com/nautechsystems/nautilus_trader), [fill models](https://nautilustrader.io/docs/latest/concepts/backtesting/fill-models/), [trade execution](https://nautilustrader.io/docs/latest/concepts/backtesting/trade-execution/)). Adapters cover Interactive Brokers, Databento, Binance, Bybit, OKX, Kraken, Coinbase and Tardis ([integrations](https://nautilustrader.io/docs/latest/integrations/)). Realism is capped by data granularity: the docs say bars "can't show intrabar order, spread, depth, or queue position," and book-based matching needs L2 or L3 deltas ([data and venues](https://nautilustrader.io/docs/latest/concepts/backtesting/data-and-venues/)). The highest-fidelity configuration is therefore L2/L3 deltas plus trade ticks plus queue position plus liquidity consumption plus a latency model, and the docs themselves call the queue estimate approximate (hidden orders and venue priority rules are not captured). Two risks need managing. First, Nautilus is mid-migration: 1.231.0 (2 Aug 2026) is the declared last Cython-core release, while the Rust-native v2 sits at **2.0.0rc7 (15 Oct 2026)** with breaking changes at nearly every release, including batched book deltas in rc7 so that "fills can differ" ([releases](https://github.com/nautechsystems/nautilus_trader/releases)); pin a version and expect churn (v2 needs `pip install --pre` and Python 3.13 or 3.14). Second, no Nautilus document states TSX support, so Canadian tick-size tables, board lots, auctions and halts must be supplied through custom instruments and data ([NautilusTrader IB docs](https://nautilustrader.io/docs/latest/integrations/interactive_brokers/)).

What a Canadian instrument definition must encode is well documented. TSX runs a 7:00 to 9:30 ET pre-open with no executions, an opening trade at 9:30, continuous trading to 16:00, a Market on Close facility from 15:50, a 16:10 to 16:15 cancel session and a 16:15 to 17:00 extended session where trades occur only at the last sale price ([TSX hours](https://tsx.com/en/trading/calendars-and-trading-hours/trading-hours)). Ticks are $0.01 at or above $0.50 and $0.005 below, with CIRO's alignment to SEC Rule 612 scheduled for **1 Nov 2027** ([CIRO proposal](https://www.ciro.ca/news-room/publications/proposed-amendments-respecting-trading-increments), [CIRO bulletin](https://www.bcsc.bc.ca/-/media/PWS/New-Resources/Industry/Marketplaces-SROs-and-Market-Infrastructure/SROs/2026/CIRO--Rules-Bulletin--Technical--Revised-Implementation-Date-for-Amendments-Respecting-Trading-Increments-June-22-2026.pdf)). Several protected venues (TSX, Nasdaq CXC, Omega, Nasdaq CX2, CSE, NEO-L, TSX Venture) compete under an order-protection rule ([CIRO order protection list](https://www.ciro.ca/news-room/publications/order-protection-rule-market-share-threshold-effective-april-1-2024)), and IBKR passes through exchange fees venue by venue ([IBKR commissions](https://www.interactivebrokers.ca/en/pricing/commissions-stocks.php)). Continuous-trading maker/taker rates, board-lot rules and TSXV/CSE spread statistics were not retrievable, so those parameters should be calibrated from your own recorded data rather than assumed.

The other engines each cover one slice. hftbacktest (MIT, 5.0k stars) is the most explicit about queue position and latency, with pluggable queue models and latency models that interpolate measured order latency, but it states that "no market impact is considered," its live bots exist only in Rust for Binance Futures and Bybit, and its latest PyPI release is 2.4.4 from 10 Dec 2025 ([GitHub](https://github.com/nkaz001/hftbacktest), [order fill docs](https://hftbacktest.readthedocs.io/en/latest/order_fill.html), [latency docs](https://hftbacktest.readthedocs.io/en/latest/latency_models.html), [PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/hftbacktest/)). It is a research tool for crypto order-book questions, not a platform. QuantConnect's LEAN defines no Canada or TSX market and defaults to zero slippage ([Market.cs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/QuantConnect/Lean/master/Common/Market.cs), [QC slippage](https://www.quantconnect.com/docs/v2/writing-algorithms/reality-modeling/slippage/key-concepts)), so TSX there means building a custom market ([QC forum](https://www.quantconnect.com/forum/discussion/9250/adding-a-complete-new-exchange-data/)). vectorbt (1.2.0, 19 Oct 2026) is a fast bar-level screener ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/vectorbt/)). Backtrader's last release was April 2023 ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/backtrader/)), ABIDES was archived on 2 Jun 2025 ([GitHub](https://github.com/jpmorganchase/abides-jpmc-public)), and Qlib's last PyPI release (0.9.7, 15 Aug 2025) supports Python only through 3.12 and needs hand-built data dumps for TSX ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/pyqlib/), [Qlib data docs](https://qlib.readthedocs.io/en/latest/component/data.html)), so treat Qlib as a source of benchmarks and feature definitions, not a foundation. Research simulators such as MarS, JAX-LOB and mbt_gym add market impact or GPU speed but none offers broker adapters, fees or live parity ([MarS](https://github.com/microsoft/MarS), [JAX-LOB](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.13289), [mbt_gym](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.07823)).

No engine closes the reactive part of the market. Vyetrenko et al. argue that historical replay cannot show how other participants respond to your orders ([Get Real](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.04941)), and a 2026 calibrated simulator finds profitability "highly sensitive to execution parameters" ([arXiv 2603.24137](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24137)). For a small account in liquid names impact is likely negligible (my judgement), and it matters more in TSXV names or illiquid altcoins. The workable posture is to treat the sim-to-real gap as a set of measured parameters: run Nautilus in Sandbox mode on live data, log simulated against observed fills, and use a small real-money pilot to learn true queue priority. The speed tier changes the requirement. Intraday and swing work on bars needs a conservative cost model and intrabar-aware stops (Freqtrade's docs warn that bar backtests show more stop-outs than live trading and recommend finer-timeframe detail, [Freqtrade](https://www.freqtrade.io/en/stable/backtesting/)). High-frequency work needs recorded L2/L3 data, queue models and measured latency, and its results hold only for orders small relative to displayed liquidity.

## Canadian exchanges are easy to research and blocked for automation, so start with crypto

### The CIRO rule closes the door on self-built orders for Canadian listings

IBKR's documentation states that Interactive Brokers Canada "does not allow users to use your own trading application to electronically submit order for products traded on a Canadian exchange or other marketplace through API," citing CIRO Dealer Member Rule 3200 A.1.(b)(i), which bars registrants from letting clients use their own automated order systems ([IBKR Web API docs](https://www.interactivebrokers.com/docs/web-api/v1/requirements-limitations/canadian-residents-restricted-from-programmatically-trading-canadian-products)). CIRO's guidance on order-execution-only accounts draws the same line and ties "automated order system" to National Instrument 23-103 ([CIRO guidance](https://www.ciro.ca/newsroom/publications/guidance-respecting-order-execution-only-accounts-form-third-party-electronic-access-marketplaces-0)). Questrade's API can read accounts and market data, but "executing trades using the API is available to Questrade partner developers only" ([Questrade API home](https://www.questrade.com/api/home)), and partner criteria are unpublished. Moomoo's OpenAPI marks Canadian stocks unsupported even for paper trading ([Futu OpenAPI](https://openapi.futunn.com/futu-api-doc/en/intro/intro.html)). Alpaca is not accepting Canadian accounts; a staff post of 14 Aug 2026 targets regulatory approval around November 2026 and never mentions TSX ([Alpaca forum](https://forum.alpaca.markets/t/is-the-market-data-subscription-available-in-canada/19519)). One undated forum thread shows an IBKR Canada account rejecting a VCN order on the TSX ("API/CTCI orders for canadian stocks are not allowed") while an AAPL order on Nasdaq went through ([QuantConnect forum](https://www.quantconnect.com/forum/discussion/7303)). Whether an IBKR paper account accepts TSX API orders from a Canadian login is untested in the evidence, so qualify a TSX contract and send a paper order on day one before designing around either answer.

What remains programmable is narrower than the priority list implies. IBKR Canada's API appears to work for US-listed securities, including US listings of Canadian interlisted names (an inference from IBKR's Canadian-exchange wording and the forum anecdote, to be verified with IBKR). The TSX leg of interlisted names is competitive and electronic, with both markets contributing to price discovery, so it is not a source of free edge ([JOIM](https://joim.com/price-discovery-cross-listed-stocks)). For TSX, TSXV and CSE names the compliant options are human-in-the-loop: the model emits signals and you place the orders, or you approve drafts through Questrade's MCP connector, where "the AI agent cannot place, change, or cancel an order on its own" ([Questrade MCP guide](https://www.questrade.com/learning/using-questrade/connect-your-questrade-account-to-ai)). Questrade Flows adds no-code if-this-then-that rules (up to 50 flows, scans every 1 to 2 seconds, a $250,000 per-order cap on the current page and a reported $10,000 cap elsewhere) but cannot run a custom ML model ([Questrade Flows](https://www.questrade.com/learning/using-questrade/automate-your-trading-strategy-with-flows-in-questrade-pro)). A daily-horizon model that produces signals after the close and orders placed by hand the next morning, with simple resting exits, is the realistic Canadian deployment. Confirm the compliance position with the broker or a securities lawyer before relying on it.

### Data for Canadian research is cheap, but tick history is not for sale to retail

Live Canadian L1 is inexpensive through IBKR: TSX CAD 9 per month and TSXV CAD 9 for non-professional Canadian residents, CSE USD 2.50 (billed in CAD for Canadian accounts), NEO USD 1.00, and TSX Level 2 about USD 14 ([IBKR Canada market data](https://www.interactivebrokers.ca/en/pricing/market-data-pricing.php)); the page carries a stray 2020 footnote, so confirm in Client Portal. Questrade's free package includes real-time L1 for TSX and TSXV ([Questrade plans](https://www.questrade.com/learning/using-questrade/real-time-streaming-plans)). IBKR paper accounts have no subscriptions of their own: they can share the live user's entitlements, but if both sessions run at once they must be on the same device or the paper session gets no data, and without sharing they receive delayed data where available ([IBKR KB 1719](https://ibkb.interactivebrokers.com/article/1719)). For daily history, Norgate's Platinum Canada package costs **CAD 630 per year** and is the only retail-priced source found that includes delisted securities and historical index constituents back to 1990 ([Norgate](https://norgatedata.com/stockmarketpackages.php)); EODHD covers 3,077 active TSX and 1,529 active TSXV tickers at $19.99 to $29.99 per month, with 15-minute delay unless indicated and no stated delisted coverage ([EODHD pricing](https://eodhd.com/pricing), [EODHD TSX](https://eodhd.com/exchange/TO)). Databento and Massive do not cover Canadian exchanges ([Databento venues](https://databento.com/docs/venues-and-datasets), [Massive docs](https://massive.com/docs/stocks/getting-started)). Depth-of-book history comes only from exchange products, for example Cboe Canada's PITCH daily file at CAD 1,000 per month ([Cboe Canada order form](https://res-certification.cboe.com/resources/membership/cboe-canada-historical-data-order-form-en.pdf)), or from recording your own feed. Check IBKR's terms on storing market data before building a training set from its stream; the notes did not verify them.

Frequent trading carries Canadian tax consequences. CRA has no bright-line test, but high frequency, short holding periods and margin financing push toward business-income treatment, which is 100% taxable and 100% deductible rather than the 50% capital-gains inclusion ([TaxTips.ca](https://www.taxtips.ca/personaltax/investing/taxtreatment/are-your-investment-gains-and-losses-capital-or-income.htm)). The superficial-loss rule denies a loss when you or an affiliated person reacquire identical property within 30 days before or after the sale, which a mean-reversion model that re-enters a ticker after a stop-out can trip across all your accounts ([RBC Wealth Management](https://ca.rbcwealthmanagement.com/documents/1435520/3126711/NAV0072+-+Superficial+loss+rules.pdf/1b4aef6b-ae5f-4125-af6e-e106b1563fee)). The notes found no primary CRA or CIRO text, so treat both points as secondary.

### Crypto and US equities compared on cost and access

| Market | Programmable access for a Canadian resident | Cheapest realistic live data | Depth history | Role in the plan |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| TSX, TSXV, CSE, Cboe Canada | No self-built orders (IBKR Canada block, Questrade partner-only); human-approved signals only | IBKR L1 CAD 9 (TSX, TSXV), USD 2.50 (CSE); Questrade free L1 | Daily: Norgate CAD 630/yr, EODHD $19.99-29.99/mo; depth: Cboe PITCH CAD 1,000/mo or self-recorded | Research and slow signals |
| US stocks | IBKR Canada API for US-listed (verify); Alpaca paper free worldwide, live not yet open to Canadians | Alpaca IEX free; Databento Standard or Massive Advanced $199/mo | Databento MBO/MBP-10, 1 month L2/L3 bundled | First real-broker paper-to-live |
| US options | Alpaca paper options; IBKR paper | OPRA via Databento (fee pass-through, prices not retrieved) | Not retrieved | Defer |
| Crypto (Coinbase, Kraken) | Registered in Canada; leverage restricted; Binance and Bybit exited in 2023 | Free public L2 (Coinbase L3 full channel) | Tardis $700-1,200/mo, or self-record | Lab for the simulator and HF research |

For US equities, Alpaca is the easy on-ramp for plumbing: anyone worldwide can open a paper-only account with an email address, but it receives only IEX data (30 websocket symbols on the free plan), and the $99-per-month SIP and OPRA plan is not sold to Canadian residents ([Alpaca paper docs](https://docs.alpaca.markets/docs/paper-trading), [Alpaca market data](https://docs.alpaca.markets/docs/about-market-data-api), [Alpaca forum](https://forum.alpaca.markets/t/is-the-market-data-subscription-available-in-canada/19519)). A consolidated feed with real depth means Databento Standard at **$199 per month** (live data, MBO and MBP-10 schemas, one year of L1 and one month of L2/L3 history bundled) or Massive Advanced at $199 (trades and quotes only) ([Databento pricing](https://databento.com/pricing), [Massive pricing](https://massive.com/pricing)). IBKR's paper account needs a funded live account, and IBKR Canada has no account minimum ([IBKR commissions](https://www.interactivebrokers.ca/en/pricing/commissions-stocks.php)). The US day-trading regime is shifting: FINRA's amendment removed the pattern-day-trader designation and the $25,000 minimum effective 4 Jun 2026, with broker phase-in allowed to 20 Oct 2027 ([FINRA Notice 26-10](https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/notices/26-10)). Options are the hardest asset to replicate, and no engine's options fidelity (assignment, expiry, Greeks) was assessed; Alpaca's paper options exclude extended hours and push no assignment events ([Alpaca options](https://docs.alpaca.markets/docs/options-trading)), so options belong in a later phase.

Crypto wins on cost and data realism. Exchanges publish full-depth books free: Binance diff-depth streams ([Binance docs](https://developers.binance.com/docs/binance-spot-api-docs/web-socket-streams)), Coinbase's no-auth full (L3) channel with rate limits of 8 requests per second per IP ([Coinbase rate limits](https://docs.cdp.coinbase.com/exchange/websocket-feed/rate-limits.md)), so recording your own L2 history costs almost nothing, whereas Tardis replay runs $700 to $1,200 per month for Solo plans ([Tardis](https://tardis.dev/#pricing)). A 24/7 market also yields 168 market hours per week against 32.5 for a regular equity session. CCXT (MIT, about 104 exchanges, `set_sandbox_mode`, CCXT Pro for websockets) gives one execution layer, though it normalises only L2 ([CCXT](https://github.com/ccxt/ccxt)). The access constraint is regulatory: Bybit stopped opening Canadian accounts on 31 May 2023 ([The Block](https://www.theblock.co/post/232602)), Binance announced its exit ([BetaKit](https://betakit.com/binance-exits-canada-as-crypto-trading-platforms-feel-broader-impact-of-stricter-regulations/)), and the OSC list shows Coinbase Canada, Kraken (Payward Canada) and Bitbuy as registered restricted dealers, as seen in a search excerpt because the OSC page blocked direct fetch ([OSC list](https://osc.ca/en/industry/registration-and-compliance/registered-crypto-asset-trading-platforms)). The same coverage notes that Canadian regulators restrict margin and leverage trading for Canadian clients, which limits the relevance of futures-style testnets. Opening Binance or Bybit demo accounts from Canada, or using a VPN, is a terms-of-service and regulatory risk. The design that avoids it is to subscribe to public mainnet streams (availability from a Canadian IP is untested) and run your own paper exchange against the live book.

My recommendation is to start with **crypto spot on Coinbase and Kraken**, as a laboratory rather than as proof that crypto is the best market. It gives the cheapest realistic data, the fastest data accumulation and no market-hours or pattern-day-trading complications. The evidence on crypto profitability is thin and secondary: a preprint reports that naive hourly BTC sign strategies fail once 10 bp costs are charged ([emergentmind](https://www.emergentmind.com/papers/2606.00060)), and survivorship and regime change are acute. US equities through IBKR come second, as the first venue with a real broker's paper-to-live path. Canadian exchanges come last for automation but remain valid for daily research and human-approved swing signals.

## Patterns repeat weakly, so build a calibrated forecaster rather than a precise one

### What the evidence says about "human patterns repeat"

Predictability is real but small. Gu, Kelly and Xiu find monthly out-of-sample R2 of **0.40%** for the best neural net against -3.46% for OLS on all features ([Gu, Kelly, Xiu](https://www.aqr.com:443/-/media/AQR/Documents/Journal-Articles/Empirical-Asset-Pricing-in-Machine-Learning.pdf?sc_lang=en)). At the daily horizon CatBoost reaches R2 of about -0.10% with 51.16% directional accuracy ([Rahimikia et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.18578v1)), and Qlib's benchmark information coefficients sit between 0.01 and 0.05 ([Qlib benchmarks](https://github.com/microsoft/qlib/tree/main/examples/benchmarks)). Patterns also decay: across 97 predictors, returns are about 26% lower out of sample and 58% lower after publication ([McLean and Pontiff](https://ivey.uwo.ca/media/3775549/pontiff.pdf)), and Fischer and Krauss's LSTM showed a 5.8 pre-cost Sharpe on S&P 500 stocks that faded to roughly zero after costs from about 2010 ([FAU paper](https://iwf.rw.fau.de/files/2015/12/11-2017.pdf)). The counter-evidence deserves weight: Chen and Zimmermann replicate 98% of 161 clearly significant predictors ([Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/open-source-cross-sectional-asset-pricing.htm)). The reading that fits both is that cross-sectional anomalies in momentum, reversal, liquidity and volatility recur on average, but with small size, time-varying strength, and a large share of profit in small, illiquid, costly stocks ([Avramov, Cheng, Metzker](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3450322)). One summary of that paper reports payoffs falling 62% once microcaps are excluded ([finews](https://www.finews.asia/finance/32603-cuhk-ai-based-stock-picking-overrated)). A deep model "learning patterns" does not escape those limits, so the system must assume decay and re-validate continuously.

This reshapes the target output. A statement such as "-0.5% in 1 hour, then +1.3% by tomorrow noon" overstates what the data supports, and the 1-hour end is the weakest: order-flow signals work only for roughly two price changes ahead and need L2 data ([Kolm, Turiel, Westray](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/mathfi/v33y2023i4p1044-1081.html), [Cont, Kukanov, Stoikov](https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.6402)), which puts them out of reach for a retail TSX or US equity stack and possible only on crypto order books (my inference). Longer horizons carry more signal, since Gu-Kelly-Xiu's annual R2 is "nearly an order of magnitude larger" than monthly, but with far fewer independent samples. Volatility is the more forecastable quantity: Kronos's small model reaches volatility R2 of 0.249 against return information coefficients near 0.04 to 0.07 ([Kronos paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.02739v1)), a correlation so small that it explains well under 1% of return variance. The usable deliverable is therefore a conditional distribution per horizon, whose mean is a small shift relative to its spread, plus a separate volatility forecast that drives sizing and stop distances. For equities, put the emphasis on daily-to-weekly cross-sectional ranking, where evidence per unit of cost is best.

### A model stack mapped to the five dimensions

| Your dimension | What to build first | Evidence and caveat |
|---|---|---|
| (1) Own history | Multi-window momentum (skipping the latest week or month), short-term reversal, realised volatility, volume and illiquidity, distance from rolling highs and lows, calendar features | GKX: all methods agree on momentum, liquidity and volatility as dominant signals ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25398)) |
| (2) Other stocks | Cross-sectional ranks and z-scores within each market, sector and market returns, beta and residual returns, BTC/ETH lagged returns for crypto; graph models later | Qlib's graph models (GATs, HIST) beat GRU or LightGBM by margins close to seed noise, and graph definitions are ad hoc (my reading of [Qlib](https://github.com/microsoft/qlib/tree/main/examples/benchmarks)) |
| (3) Regime | Statistical jump model plus a volatility state, used as a feature or a position-size gate; never a full-sample HMM | Jump models beat HMM-guided strategies with costs and delay ([Shu, Yu, Mulvey](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.05272v3)); reversal returns vary strongly with VIX ([Nagel](https://nber.org/papers/w17653)) |
| (4) Everything affecting everything | GBDT interactions first; iTransformer or TSMixer (NeuralForecast) and Chronos-2 group attention as challengers | Trees and NNs win in GKX through nonlinear interactions; no standard benchmark of relation-graph types exists |
| (5) Continuous multi-horizon with confidence | Quantile-loss GBDT heads per horizon, conformalised with adaptive conformal inference; volatility-normalised forward log-returns as targets | ACI gives long-run, marginal coverage under shift, not per-step guarantees ([Gibbs and Candès](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2021/hash/0d441de75945e5acbc865406fc9a2559-Abstract.html)) |

Model choice follows the evidence on financial returns. Trees stay state of the art on medium-sized tabular data ([Grinsztajn et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.08815)), simple linear models beat Transformer forecasters on nine long-horizon datasets ([Zeng et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.13504)), and in Qlib's Alpha360 run the Transformer's IC was 0.0114 with a negative return against 0.0400 for LightGBM ([Qlib benchmarks](https://github.com/microsoft/qlib/tree/main/examples/benchmarks)). Zero-shot time-series foundation models fare worse on returns: Chronos-large posts out-of-sample R2 of -1.37% and TimesFM-500M -2.80% against about -0.10% for CatBoost, and only finance-specific pre-training narrows the gap (51.74% directional accuracy against 51.16%), all before transaction costs ([Rahimikia et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.18578v1)). A June 2026 test on five liquid US stocks found only 16 pretrained model-asset cases with positive skill against a zero-return random walk ([Noguer i Alonso and Franklin](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.27100)). Kronos (MIT licence, small and base weights open) shows promise but its numbers are author-run, mostly on Chinese A-shares, and its README calls the pipeline "a demonstration" ([Kronos](https://github.com/shiyu-coder/Kronos)). TimesFM 3.0 weights carry a non-commercial licence, whereas 2.5 and earlier are Apache-2.0 ([TimesFM](https://github.com/google-research/timesfm)). So the recipe is LightGBM or CatBoost first, with foundation models as baselines or feature generators through Darts' wrappers ([Darts](https://github.com/unit8co/darts)). A Canadian-stock write-up reports the same pattern, with XGBoost and mid-depth networks beating deeper ones and gains concentrated in small-cap and value names, but it is an unreviewed blog summary with no cost treatment ([CFA Institute](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/blogs/enterprising-investor/2026/machine-learning-improves-forecasting)).

### Validation is where solo projects fail

Because published edges are small and plentiful, the protocol matters more than the architecture. Use time-ordered splits only, purge by label horizon plus an embargo at least as long as the longest feature lookback, fit scalers and rank transforms inside each fold, and use skfolio's `CombinatorialPurgedCV` to obtain a distribution of out-of-sample paths ([skfolio](https://skfolio.org/generated/skfolio.model_selection.CombinatorialPurgedCV.html)). Log every model, feature and hyperparameter trial and report the Deflated Sharpe Ratio with N equal to the trials run ([Bailey and López de Prado](https://www.davidhbailey.com/dhbpapers/deflated-sharpe.pdf)), plus the Probability of Backtest Overfitting ([Bailey et al.](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2326253)). Harvey, Liu and Zhu argue new factors should clear t above 3.0 ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20592)). Include a zero-forecast and a simple linear baseline in every table. Use delisting-inclusive universes, which matters most for TSXV. If a pretrained model is used, confirm its pre-training cutoff precedes your test window, as Kronos does. As planning heuristics rather than cited facts, treat a net Sharpe of 0.5 to 1.0 as a good outcome and any pre-cost Sharpe above about 2 on a frequently rebalanced portfolio as a leakage or capacity artefact, as with Rahimikia's 6.79 pre-cost headline.

## Forecast-then-decide beats RL as the core, and RL earns a place only as an overlay

### The evidence against RL as the alpha engine

RL's appeal is real: you need a replica to explore safely, and Moody and Saffell showed in 1998 that systems trained directly on profit or differential Sharpe beat an MSE-trained forecaster on monthly S&P 500 data, because optimal decisions under costs depend on current position ([NIPS 1998](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/1551-reinforcement-learning-for-trading)). Modern evidence is less kind. A 2026 critical survey of 124 papers found 86.3% report outperformance with no formal statistical test and 89.5% model no transaction costs or only a flat fee ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=7363738)). A July 2026 walk-forward study of learned allocation policies on 16 CME futures found the best model's Sharpe of 0.55 "matches rather than significantly beats" equal-weight at 0.52, and an LSTM's net Sharpe fell from 0.50 to -0.38 as costs rose from 0 to 10 bp ([arXiv 2607.00475](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.00475v1)). TensorTrade's README reports a PPO agent on BTC/USD earning +$239 at 0% commission but losing $650 at 0.1% against buy-and-hold at -$355, concluding that "commission costs currently exceed prediction profits" ([TensorTrade](https://github.com/tensortrade-org/tensortrade)). The sample problem compounds this: a stock-year holds about 252 daily bars, and GPU environments reaching about 227,000 samples per second in the FinRL contests make throughput cheap but not diversity ([FinRL Contests](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.02281v4)). RL's documented industry success sits in order execution and market making, where the agent affects outcomes and rewards are dense, as in JPMorgan's LOXM and Qlib's RL order-execution module ([Hambly, Xu, Yang](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2112.04553), [MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/f/608467/jpmorgan-will-use-ai-to-carry-out-trades-across-the-world/), [Qlib](https://github.com/microsoft/qlib)). No audited live deep-RL alpha deployment turned up in the notes.

The state-dependence argument is right, but classical multi-period control already captures it. Gârleanu and Pedersen show that with costs and predictors that decay at different speeds, the optimal policy trades partway toward an "aim" portfolio blending current and expected future targets, weighting slower-decaying signals more ([NBER w15205](https://www.nber.org/papers/w15205)), and Boyd et al. solve the numerical version as receding-horizon convex optimization that executes only the first trade ([arXiv 1705.00109](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1705.00109)). That is the "hold today, sell tomorrow noon" behaviour you described, produced by an auditable optimizer instead of a policy network. The steelman for hybrid designs is that a forecast in the RL state can help, but the one test found was in energy markets and still needed raw price context ([arXiv 2606.27032](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.27032)), so the hybrid evidence base is thin.

### What the decision layer actually does

Compute net edge per asset as the decay-weighted sum of horizon forecasts minus expected round-trip cost (spread, fees, slippage scaled by size and volatility), shrunk toward zero by forecast uncertainty. Your worked example then becomes a rule: sell ahead of the predicted dip and re-enter only if the avoided 0.5% loss minus round-trip cost exceeds the probability-weighted risk that the +1.3% move arrives early. For liquid US large caps costs of a few basis points make tactical trimming possible, whereas in TSXV names or small altcoins costs can exceed the dip and the correct action is to hold, a conclusion the notes mark as reasoning rather than a tested result. Size with fractional Kelly, perhaps 0.1 to 0.5 of full (my judgement). The Kelly formula is fragile: a 6% excess return at 15% volatility gives a 267% Kelly fraction, and errors in estimated means cost far more than errors in covariances ([Kelly criterion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelly_criterion), [arXiv 2508.18868](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.18868v2)). Overlay volatility targeting, which lifts Sharpe mainly for equity-like risk assets but reduces tail risk across the board ([Quantpedia summary of Harvey et al.](https://quantpedia.com/the-impact-of-volatility-targeting-on-equities-bonds-commodities-and-currencies)), and cap exposure per name, sector and asset class.

For stop-loss logic the evidence is conditional. Under a random walk, simple stop rules reduce expected return, while with momentum or regime switching they can add value ([Kaminski and Lo](https://dspace.mit.edu/entities/publication/bb69ca4b-0cdc-487f-831d-63b2e84fafee)); a mean-reverting short-horizon forecaster can be stopped out just before its expected rebound (my inference). Prefer horizon-matched time stops, a thesis-invalidated exit when the updated forecast flips sign net of costs, a position loss limit sized to the risk budget, and a portfolio drawdown breaker that scales exposure toward a floor in the Grossman-Zhou style, with a cooldown to avoid whipsaw ([Grossman and Zhou](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/mathfi/v3y1993i3p241-276.html)). A supervised meta-labeling gate (a LightGBM secondary model on triple-barrier labels, with calibrated probabilities scaling or vetoing the size) is a plausible add-on, but its out-of-sample evidence is modest, so require that it improve net Deflated Sharpe before keeping it ([Jesse docs](https://docs.jesse.trade/docs/research/ml/meta-labeling)). Finally, the risk gate must sit outside the model: Knight Capital's $12M SEC settlement cited missing pre-submission controls and limit checks ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/news/press-release/2013-222)), so enforce maximum order size, per-name and gross exposure, price-band checks, order-rate throttles, a daily loss limit, a stale-data watchdog, a one-command kill switch and broker position reconciliation.

### Where RL fits

RL belongs in two roles. The first is a constrained overlay: a MaskablePPO or RecurrentPPO policy from SB3-Contrib (SB3 2.9.0, June 2026) whose state holds forecast quantiles by horizon, position, unrealised PnL, time in trade and cost features, and whose actions only scale exposure within the optimizer's cap or exit ([SB3-Contrib](https://sb3-contrib.readthedocs.io/en/master/)). Reward should be net PnL after modelled costs minus a drawdown or turnover penalty, trained with randomised costs, latency and partial fills over multiple seeds, run in shadow mode, and promoted only if it beats the identical pipeline without RL net of costs on purged out-of-sample data. Gort et al.'s hypothesis-test approach to rejecting overfitted agents is a useful filter, though in one FinRL 2025 contest entry the filtered PPO agent still returned -34.96% while the crypto index fell 50.78% ([Gort et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.05559), [FinRL Contests](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.02281v4)). Write a small custom Gymnasium environment instead of inheriting FinRL (PyPI 0.3.7, April 2024, now pointing users to FinRL-X), gym-anytrading (no hold action) or TensorTrade (constant costs). The second role is execution, if high-frequency work becomes the goal: mbt_gym or JAX-LOB for fast policy prototyping, validated in Nautilus or hftbacktest ([mbt_gym](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.07823), [JAX-LOB](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.13289)). Even FinRL-X's best live-style evidence, +19.76% against SPY's -2.51% over about 4.5 months, came from two rule-based strategies in a weight-vector pipeline, and the authors disclaim statistical significance ([FinRL-X](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.21330v1)).

## A phased plan takes you from recorded data to tiny live capital

### The recommended end-to-end architecture

The architecture separates concerns around one narrow interface, target portfolio weights, which FinRL-X also uses so backtest and live consume the same representation ([FinRL-X](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.21330v1)).

## Conclusion

The central finding is that the simulator and the validation protocol are the product, and the model is the replaceable part. With out-of-sample R2 below 1%, decaying anomalies and a sim-to-real gap that no engine closes, the durable assets are the recorder, a replay engine whose fill, latency and fee parameters are measured against real fills, and a purged, trial-counted evaluation process. A retail-scale team should expect a good outcome to be a net Sharpe of 0.5 to 1.0, and treat anything far above that as a bug.

The regulatory structure also inverts your market priorities in a useful way. Canada's rule against self-built orders removes automation from TSX, TSXV, CSE and Cboe Canada, yet it leaves the slow end of the stack, daily or weekly signals placed by hand or approved through a broker, where the model's low edge is least damaged by costs and latency. Programmable markets are the ones where fast decisions are least likely to pay for a solo developer, so a single codebase that trades crypto and US names automatically and emits approved signals for Canadian names is a coherent end state rather than a compromise. Three things would change this advice: a Canadian broker opening a self-service order API, Alpaca's Canadian launch bringing consolidated US data, and a hands-on test showing IBKR paper accepts TSX orders; none is confirmed today. The evidence base here comes from documentation, papers and secondary summaries rather than live trials, so Phase 0 and Phase 5 are the points where reality should overrule this report.
